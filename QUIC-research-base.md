# **Domain Exploration and Literature Review: QUIC Performance Under Mobile Handover**

## **Introduction to the Protocol and Mobility Domain**

The evolution of transport-layer protocols has historically been constrained by the rigid architecture of the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) and its subsequent integration with Transport Layer Security (TLS). Designed for a wired, stationary internet, TCP binds communication sessions to a strict four-tuple: the source IP address, source port, destination IP address, and destination port1. In modern network environments—characterized by ubiquitous mobility, fluctuating signal strengths, and frequent switching between Wireless Fidelity (Wi-Fi) and Fifth-Generation (5G) cellular arrays—this static binding introduces severe architectural limitations3.  
The introduction of the Quick UDP Internet Connections (QUIC) protocol, standardized in 2021 as RFC 9000 by the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), represents a fundamental paradigm shift in telecommunications1. Operating entirely in user space over the User Datagram Protocol (UDP), QUIC decouples the cryptographic and transport session state from the underlying physical network address, paving the way for revolutionary capabilities in mobile environments1.

### **Demystifying the Domain: A Layman’s Analogy**

To fully comprehend the domain of "QUIC Performance under Mobile Handover," it is helpful to conceptualize internet data transmission as a secure logistics and courier delivery service operating in a rapidly changing, densely populated city.  
**The Legacy Courier (TCP):** Imagine a highly bureaucratic courier (representing TCP) tasked with delivering a multi-part encyclopedia to a customer. Before handing over the first book, the courier insists on a lengthy verification process, checking the customer's exact physical address, identification, and security clearance. This mirrors the TCP 3-way handshake combined with a separate TLS encryption handshake, which takes significant time (multiple Round-Trip Times, or RTTs) before any actual data is delivered1. Furthermore, this courier is rigidly tied to the customer's initial physical address. If the customer steps out of their home network and walks across the street to a coffee shop—representing a mobile network handover from Wi-Fi to a cellular network—their physical IP address changes8. The TCP courier immediately declares the contract void, drops the remaining books, forces the customer to sign a brand-new contract at the coffee shop, and restarts the entire cryptographic verification process. This structural rigidity causes severe delays, stalled downloads, and interrupted video streams, which developers historically attempted to mask with massive application-layer buffers9.  
**The Modern Ninja Courier (QUIC):** QUIC operates as a modern, highly efficient courier. During the very first encounter, this courier simultaneously verifies the customer's identity, establishes a secure encryption lockbox, and assigns the customer a unique, permanent "Customer ID" badge (the Connection ID)5. Because the setup process is unified, it happens exponentially faster, often achieving 1-RTT or even 0-RTT (zero round-trip time) for returning clients7.  
Crucially, if the customer moves from their home to the coffee shop, the QUIC courier does not panic. When the customer arrives at the new location, they simply flash their unique Customer ID badge10. The courier recognizes the badge, instantly verifies that the new location is safe and bidirectional (a process known as Path Validation), and resumes throwing books exactly where they left off, without negotiating a new contract11. Furthermore, if one specific book is lost in transit, the QUIC courier only pauses the delivery of that specific chapter, continuing to deliver other independent chapters simultaneously. This eliminates the "head-of-line blocking" problem that plagues TCP, where one lost packet halts the entire connection7.  
The domain of "QUIC Performance under Mobile Handover" studies exactly this transition period. The research asks critical questions: *When the customer moves, how quickly does the QUIC courier recognize the new location? What happens to the packets dropped in transit during the physical radio switch? How does the courier adjust their throwing speed (congestion control) when they realize the coffee shop network is far more congested than the home network?*

### **Technical Definition of Mobile Handover in QUIC**

In technical terms, a mobile handover (or handoff) occurs when User Equipment (UE) transitions its physical layer connection from one access node to another. This transition can be horizontal, such as switching between two 5G base stations, or vertical, such as switching from an 802.11 Wi-Fi access point to a 5G mmWave cellular tower3.  
Under legacy TCP architectures, a vertical handover inherently changes the device's IP address. This destroys the TCP four-tuple, forcing a connection reset, flushing the congestion window, and mandating an entirely new TLS cryptographic handshake8.  
QUIC mitigates this via a feature specifically engineered for mobility: **Connection Migration**. The protocol identifies sessions using a 64-bit Connection ID (CID) rather than the IP/Port tuple10. When the physical IP changes, the QUIC endpoint initiates a two-step migration process11:

1. **Path Probing (Validation):** The client sends a PATH\_CHALLENGE frame containing unpredictable cryptographic entropy over the new network interface. The server must echo this entropy in a PATH\_RESPONSE frame, proving that the new path is bidirectional and defending against IP spoofing or traffic amplification attacks9.  
2. **Migration Execution:** Once validated, the client seamlessly shifts the transmission of STREAM frames to the new path. To prevent on-path eavesdroppers from linking the user's old and new IP addresses (thereby preserving user privacy), the client utilizes a fresh, pre-negotiated CID for the new path2.

While the theoretical design of QUIC connection migration is flawless, the real-world performance during mobile handovers remains highly volatile. Variables such as path asymmetry, out-of-order packet delivery, middlebox interference (NAT rebindings and stateful firewalls), and the reaction of complex congestion control algorithms to sudden latency spikes dictate the actual Quality of Experience (QoE)14.

### **Category 1: Empirical Measurements and Real-World Deployment**

The foundational layer of QUIC research involves measuring its baseline performance against TCP in varied physical environments. A 2024 empirical comparative analysis highlighted that while TCP achieves higher raw throughput in pristine environments (due to decades of kernel-space optimizations), QUIC exhibits vastly superior latency stability under degraded conditions typical of mobile handovers23. The researchers subjected QUIC and TCP to varied network conditions, introducing up to 30ms of artificial delay and 1-2% packet loss. They discovered that in clean networks, TCP achieved approximately 5.3 Gbps compared to QUIC's 0.20 Gbps—a discrepancy attributed to QUIC's user-space computational overhead, specifically related to framing and TLS encryption1. However, as packet loss spiked, TCP's throughput became highly volatile and its 95th percentile (p95) latency spiked severely due to congestion window reductions. Conversely, QUIC's multi-stream architecture maintained a flat, stable throughput and latency profile, confirming that QUIC sacrifices peak raw speed for extreme reliability and connection stability in fluctuating mobile environments9.  
To isolate the impact of connection migration, a 2024 study conducted by Telkom University measured video streaming Quality of Experience (QoE) during physical transitions between Wi-Fi access points across different frequency bands (2.4 GHz to 5 GHz)26. The results were stark: QUIC maintained latency below 0.14 ms during the handover window and reduced packet loss by up to 6.39% compared to TCP26. Because QUIC utilized its 0-RTT initiation and CID persistence, the throughput curve showed near-instantaneous restoration on the target access point, whereas TCP required retransmission and duplicate ACKs, causing multi-second buffering events6. Furthermore, geographical studies, such as a 2025 analysis of QUIC deployment in Vietnam, utilized ZMap to scan the regional IPv4 space, discovering that while absolute deployment remains in its infancy locally (\~0.05% of addresses), active measurement testbeds revealed that in real-world Vietnamese cellular and Wi-Fi networks, QUIC consistently achieved a lower Time To First Byte (TTFB) and higher overall throughput than TCP29.  
However, the most significant and disruptive empirical study in the past five years was published in ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review in 202528. Buchet and Pelsser conducted the first Internet-wide scanning study to quantify the actual deployment and server-side support of QUIC's connection migration feature. While the theoretical benefits of QUIC migration are well documented, the authors developed a novel migration-testing client tool and discovered a crucial reality check: despite a massive 5x growth in general QUIC adoption over the past two years, support for the Connection Migration extension is dismally low28. The majority of high-profile web destinations and content providers actively drop QUIC connections if the client's IP address changes28. The paper posits that security concerns, load-balancer architecture limitations, and middlebox ossification are actively preventing the real-world utilization of this critical mobility feature28.

### **Category 2: Multipath QUIC (MPQUIC) and Advanced Scheduling**

Standard QUIC is designed for single-path operation, moving from one path to another sequentially during a handover. However, the most active area of transport research is Multipath QUIC (MPQUIC), which enables simultaneous data transmission across multiple physical interfaces (e.g., combining 5G and Wi-Fi bandwidth)33. The foundational architecture for this was formalized in 2026 via the IETF Internet-Draft "Managing multiple paths for a QUIC connection"33. The core novelty of this standard-track draft is the mandate that each physical path must maintain a distinct, isolated packet number state and Path ID33. This isolation is critical because different physical paths have varying latencies; a unified packet counter would trigger false-positive loss detections (spurious Fast Retransmits) when packets from a slower link arrive later than packets from a faster link16.  
With the protocol framing formalized, researchers have focused on the complex task of scheduling packets across heterogeneous networks. A 2023 study in the journal Computer Science and Information Systems (CSIS) formulated a mathematical path selection strategy for MPQUIC optimized specifically to reduce the Flow Completion Time (FCT) of large data transfers20. Tested via Mininet emulations using the Abilene topology, their custom scheduler demonstrated a superior cumulative distribution function (CDF) for FCT, resulting in drastically lower path delays compared to standard Lowest-RTT-First (LRF) or Pluginized QUIC (PQUIC) baselines20.  
For real-time applications, video streaming is highly sensitive to the out-of-order packet delivery common in multipath environments. To address this, a 2023 paper introduced 4D-MAP, incorporating four novel scheduling mechanisms: Dispatch, Duplicate, Discard, and Decompensate22. The algorithm leverages contextual bandit learning (LinUCB) to conquer path heterogeneity and bandwidth degradation22. Evaluated in both controlled and real-world networks, 4D-MAP vastly improved the QoE for live video, proving that adaptive, AI-driven MPQUIC schedulers are necessary to mask the physical disruptions of network handovers22.  
Scaling these concepts to extreme environments, a 2026 study addressed the deployment of MPQUIC in UAV-Assisted Space-Air-Ground Integrated Networks (SAGINs)31. Deploying MPQUIC across Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) and Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellites introduces catastrophic out-of-order delivery issues due to massive path asymmetry and high-speed multi-access contention31. The authors introduced the GPASP framework, which utilizes a probabilistic self-predictive latent encoder to schedule packets. By dynamically learning long-range temporal correlations, GPASP filters out noise caused by transient UAV handovers, stabilizing at approximately 12 Mbps where traditional heuristic algorithms failed at less than 3 Mbps31.  
Moving away from software emulators, an industrial-grade implementation of Multipath QUIC (MPTQUIC, based on Tencent's TQUIC framework) was deployed and evaluated in a dual-5G setup for Future Railway Mobile Communication System (FRMCS) scenarios in 202632. Benchmarked on physical 5G modems using adaptive scheduling and cross-path retransmission mechanisms, the protocol achieved 1.9x higher throughput and a 90% reduction in packet loss during artificially induced physical path failures, maintaining sub-millisecond jitter32. This hardware validation confirms that MPQUIC is mature enough to replace specialized legacy protocols in mission-critical high-speed mobility scenarios32.

### **Category 3: Congestion Control and Queue Management**

Congestion control is arguably the most volatile variable during a mobile handover. When a UE shifts from a home Wi-Fi network to a congested public cellular network, the available bandwidth on the new path is entirely unknown. Standard congestion control algorithms, such as NewReno or Cubic, rely on packet loss to detect congestion, which leads to suboptimal performance immediately following a handover15.  
To solve this, a 2023 study introduced PBQ-Enhanced QUIC, fusing the Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time (BBR) algorithm with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) technique21. In PBQ-enhanced QUIC, the PPO agent continuously monitors the network state and dynamically outputs optimal Congestion Window (CWnd) sizes, while the underlying BBR algorithm governs the client's pacing rate21. Experimental results revealed that this AI-driven approach significantly outperforms traditional QUIC implementations, particularly in maintaining throughput stability and minimizing Round-Trip Time (RTT) immediately following a sudden change in path latency21.  
At the edge of the network, consumer IoT devices with limited processing capabilities face a different challenge: buffer bloat. When network quality drops during a physical handover, incoming QUIC streams can cause rapid buffer expansion in resource-constrained devices, defeating the protocol's low-latency design. A 2024 study proposed a queue management algorithm (PID-Delay) inspired by Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) control theory to manage QUIC data flows27. The PID-Delay algorithm predicts queue error trends by adding differential operations to traditional PI control, preemptively adjusting buffer allocations27. Simulation results showcased an average goodput increase of 1.98 times and a 26.1% reduction in delay standard deviation compared to classical queue management algorithms, vastly improving QUIC's viability on lightweight edge hardware27.

### **Category 4: Predictive Mobility and Architecture Enhancements**

Historically, cellular handovers are event-driven, relying on instantaneous drops in the Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI)4. A 2024 paper argued that reactive handovers fail in beyond-5G (B5G) networks due to ultra-dense small-cell deployment. The authors developed an ML-assisted Predictive Conditional Handover (P-CHO) framework using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks to forecast signal quality and steer QUIC connections proactively across Multi-Radio Access Technologies (Multi-RAT)25. By training LSTM models on user mobility trajectories to predict future Signal-to-Interference-Plus-Noise Ratios (SINR) for both Wi-Fi and 5G, the system anticipates degradation25. Integrated with a Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) controller, the system executes QUIC connection migrations *before* the primary link fails (make-before-break). This approach practically eliminates the ping-pong effect and prevents the throughput cliff typically observed in reactive handover schemes25.  
From a transport-layer perspective, Kim and Koh proposed mQUIC in 2023, a handover detection mechanism that operates entirely independently of link-layer triggers18. Because typical QUIC implementations require cross-layer signaling from the OS/link-layer to trigger a connection migration, the authors proposed relying entirely on transport-layer signals18. When a handover occurs, in-flight data results in an ENETUNREACH network error18. The mQUIC client detects this error, queries the routing table for a new IP, and instantly begins the PATH\_CHALLENGE process18. For silent connections with no active data, a Handover Detection Timer (HDT) is utilized18. Real-world testbed experiments demonstrated significant performance gains over legacy break-before-make handovers, preserving session continuity without requiring proprietary OS-level radio APIs18.  
Further architectural enhancements include the 2024 Q-MOFI proposal, which integrates QUIC into a Mobile-Oriented Future Internet architecture that inherently separates Host Identity from Location (ID-LOC separation)24. Utilizing QUIC’s connection identifiers alongside a distributed mapping system, the architecture drastically reduces the binding operation latency required for a moving host to register its new location in the network24. Experimental validation demonstrated that Q-MOFI vastly outperforms traditional UDP-based MOFI models, providing seamless throughput continuity as the number of roaming hosts scales24. Additionally, an early 2021 Ericsson patent introduced a server-side control mechanism for QUIC migration between neighboring Content Delivery Network (CDN) servers sharing the same IP address17. By transferring the context state (including static keys and stateless reset tokens) between edge servers, the backend server processing the data physically shifts as the user moves, maintaining the CID and enabling seamless edge-node handovers without alerting the client17.

### **Category 5: Edge-Case Scenarios and Protocol Tunnels**

Research has also explored unconventional applications of QUIC in mobile and constrained environments. A 2023 study explored decentralized Earthquake Early Warning (EEW) systems where sensor nodes must exchange data directly19. The authors utilized QUIC encapsulated in a hole-punched UDP tunnel to bypass Network Address Translation (NAT) restrictions, acting as an alternative to Software-Defined Wide Area Networks (SD-WAN)19. Stripping QUIC out of its standard Client-Server HTTP/3 paradigm and applying it to peer-to-peer (P2P) IoT sensor networks proved highly novel. The performance evaluation proved that QUIC outperforms TCP over ZeroTier in terms of latency and retransmission efficiency, primarily due to QUIC's elimination of head-of-line blocking, which ensured that genuine seismic alerts were processed independently from false-alarm verifications19.  
Recognizing that protocol ossification makes it difficult to upgrade legacy enterprise software to HTTP/3, a 2024/2025 study investigated the encapsulation of legacy TCP traffic within a TCP-over-QUIC tunneling proxy1. During experimental evaluation with up to 20% induced packet loss—simulating a severely degraded mobile handover boundary—the tunneled TCP traffic maintained significantly higher throughput than native TCP1. While modest computational overhead was observed in ideal conditions, the tunnel artificially granted legacy applications the mobility and loss-recovery benefits of QUIC1.  
Finally, a 2026 IETF Internet-Draft authored by Masahiro Kozuka proposed an extension for Server-Initiated Connection Migration30. Reversing the established RFC 9000 paradigm where only the client can initiate migration, this draft defines the allow\_server\_migration transport parameter30. This addresses specific topological edge cases where a mobile client possesses a public IP address while the server resides behind a volatile NAT. By negotiating an empty transport parameter during the handshake, the server can dynamically optimize its routing path post-handshake, cutting latency and bypassing intermediate relay nodes30.

## **Second and Third-Order Thematic Insights**

Analyzing the massive influx of empirical data, algorithmic proposals, and protocol drafts published between 2021 and 2026 uncovers several deep, cross-sectional trends that extend far beyond isolated network metrics. Synthesizing these findings reveals the underlying forces shaping the future of mobile transport protocols.

### **1\. The Disconnect Between Theoretical Design and Middlebox Ossification**

A profound contradiction exists at the heart of current QUIC research. On one hand, advanced academic studies are successfully deploying AI-driven, multi-path QUIC across stratospheric UAVs and mission-critical high-speed rail networks, achieving near-perfect session continuity and massive throughput gains31. On the other hand, empirical internet-wide scanning reveals that the fundamental feature enabling this mobility—Connection Migration—is actively blocked or unsupported by the vast majority of commercial internet servers and load balancers28.  
This systemic failure suggests that protocol ossification is no longer limited to the transport layer (TCP). Middleboxes, stateful firewalls, and edge load balancers (such as HAProxy configurations) are now ossifying around QUIC2. Firewalls trained to drop unrecognized UDP packets routinely block migrating CIDs, viewing sudden IP shifts without a corresponding handshake as spoofing attacks or anomalous behavior2. This implies that theoretical enhancements to QUIC's congestion or scheduling algorithms are effectively marooned in academic testbeds unless they are paired with advancements in NAT traversal resilience and middlebox negotiation. Future implementations must prioritize fallback mechanisms or tunneling strategies to gracefully handle environments where migration is silently dropped by the network edge.

### **2\. The Dissolution of the OSI Model via Predictive Cross-Layer Handovers**

Historically, cellular networks operate strictly within their designated layers. Handovers are triggered reactively at the physical and link layers: when the RSSI drops below a critical threshold, the modem switches4. The transport protocol (Layer 4\) is entirely blind to this physical transition, suffering the consequences passively via packet loss and catastrophic congestion window collapse.  
The literature indicates a massive paradigm shift toward Machine Learning-assisted Predictive Conditional Handover (P-CHO)25. By utilizing Recurrent Neural Networks (like LSTMs) to track user trajectories and forecast signal decay in Multi-RAT environments, the network control plane can inform the QUIC layer *before* the physical link breaks25. The QUIC protocol, equipped with this foresight, can proactively initiate path probing on a secondary interface and seamlessly migrate the stream. The third-order implication is the total dissolution of rigid OSI model boundaries. Optimal mobility in dense 5G/6G environments now requires a continuous, neural feedback loop between the Radio Access Network (RAN) and the User-Space Transport Layer. Transport protocols can no longer operate as agnostic overlays; they must become context-aware entities integrated into the edge-computing fabric.

### **3\. The Mathematical Limits of Heuristic Congestion Control**

As Multipath QUIC (MPQUIC) matures, researchers consistently highlight the challenge of scheduling packets across highly asymmetric links22. When a mobile device simultaneously aggregates a fast, highly volatile 5G mmWave connection with a slow, stable 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi connection, packets inevitably arrive at the receiver out of order3.  
Standard heuristic congestion control algorithms (like NewReno, Cubic, or even basic BBR) are structurally incapable of handling extreme asymmetry. They misinterpret this out-of-order delivery as network loss, triggering spurious Fast Retransmits and unnecessarily slashing the congestion window6. While the IETF's mandate for isolated packet numbering spaces per path partially mitigates this33, the broader trend is the wholesale replacement of heuristic algorithms with Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents21. The development of PBQ-enhanced QUIC and 4D-MAP demonstrates that static algorithms are reaching their mathematical limits in heterogeneous networks21. Future transport protocols will be entirely reliant on dynamically trained AI agents that can balance latency, buffer bloat, and throughput in real-time, learning the unique topological constraints of the local RF environment rather than relying on generalized, hard-coded rules.

## **Problem Statement Definition and Novelty Framing**

### **Synthesized Problem Statement**

The exhaustive literature review establishes a robust foundation for defining the core problem statement of the capstone project.  
While the fundamental mechanics of QUIC's Connection Migration and Multipath extensions theoretically eliminate the latency penalties and session breakage inherent to TCP during mobile handovers7, their practical efficacy in real-world networks is severely degraded by two interacting variables: **Middlebox Ossification** and **Congestion Control Collapse under Path Asymmetry**.  
Specifically, when a mobile device executes a physical handover across highly asymmetric Radio Access Technologies (e.g., from a high-bandwidth 5G link to a legacy Wi-Fi network), legacy congestion control algorithms misinterpret the resulting out-of-order packet delivery and sudden latency spikes as network congestion, leading to a catastrophic collapse of the congestion window6. Furthermore, attempts to seamlessly migrate the Connection ID are frequently blocked by commercial NATs and load balancers that do not support QUIC path validation2.  
Therefore, the problem is defined as follows: *How can QUIC maintain high throughput and session continuity during vertical mobile handovers in highly asymmetric Multi-RAT environments, given the constraints of legacy heuristic congestion control and the systemic lack of middlebox support for connection migration?*

### **Objective Definition and Novelty Framing**

To inject highly unique and impactful novelty into the capstone paper, the research objectives must pivot away from basic throughput benchmarking (a domain that is already heavily saturated with literature23) and focus squarely on resolving the transport-layer disconnect during the handover transition window.  
**Proposed Novelty Path 1: Context-Aware Heuristic Congestion Control for Asymmetric Handovers** While existing literature relies heavily on Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) to manage congestion during handovers21, DRL is computationally expensive and difficult to deploy on resource-constrained mobile devices. A highly novel objective would be to design and evaluate a lightweight, context-aware modification to the BBR algorithm specifically for single-path QUIC migration. This modification would utilize the PATH\_CHALLENGE phase to explicitly signal physical path asymmetry (e.g., passing radio-layer RSSI metrics directly to the QUIC user-space implementation). This cross-layer signaling would allow the client to preemptively freeze or intelligently scale the congestion window *before* the out-of-order packets arrive, mitigating the post-handover throughput cliff without the overhead of neural networks.  
**Proposed Novelty Path 2: Graceful Migration Fallback Mechanisms** Addressing the empirical reality that commercial servers actively drop migrating QUIC connections28, a second novel objective involves designing a graceful fallback protocol. The research could evaluate a mechanism where the QUIC client, upon detecting a failed PATH\_RESPONSE during a mobile handover (indicating a middlebox block), instantly encapsulates the QUIC session state within a secondary, short-lived TCP tunnel just long enough to re-establish a native UDP route. Measuring the QoE impact of this "hybrid fallback" approach would provide a highly practical and immediately impactful contribution to the current IETF standardization efforts.  
By utilizing these findings as a base, the capstone project is positioned to move beyond theoretical protocol emulation and address the precise friction points preventing the seamless deployment of next-generation mobile transport architectures.
